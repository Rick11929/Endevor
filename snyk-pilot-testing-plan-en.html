1. Background & Objectives

We have decided to run an internal pilot of Snyk. Because the Snyk Broker service (required for connecting Snyk to our internal/private repositories behind the firewall) cannot be deployed in the corporate intranet in the short term, all Broker-dependent capabilities are deferred to Phase 2. This first pilot phase is therefore limited to Snyk CLI and IDE plugin workflows, which operate from the developer workstation and do not depend on the Broker.

In addition, this pilot covers three Snyk capabilities only: SAST (Snyk Code), SCA (Open Source), and IaC. Other Snyk products and capabilities — including Snyk Container, license compliance, and reporting/governance features — are not part of this pilot.

1.1 Objectives

  • Validate core value: confirm Snyk can accurately detect vulnerabilities (SCA), code issues (SAST), and misconfigurations (IaC) in our real internal codebases via CLI and IDE.
  • Evaluate developer experience: measure installation friction, scan speed, result readability, fix workflow, finding triage workflow, and false-positive burden in day-to-day development.
  • Verify internal-network compatibility: confirm CLI/IDE work within our corporate network (proxy, firewall, internal package registries such as Nexus/Artifactory, internal CA certificates).
  • Collect adoption signals: understand whether developers would keep using Snyk after the pilot, before we invest in Broker deployment and broader rollout.
  • Produce a go/no-go recommendation for Phase 2 (Broker deployment + SCM/CI integration) with concrete data.

Appendix B — NPS & SUS Explained (Definitions & Calculation)

Two standardized instruments are used in the W4 survey to quantify subjective experience. Both are industry-standard, benchmarked methods — using them lets us compare Snyk's scores against published norms instead of relying on ad-hoc "did you like it?" questions.

B.1 NPS — Net Promoter Score

What it is: NPS is a loyalty/advocacy metric created by Fred Reichheld (Bain & Company, 2003). It measures whether users would actively recommend the product — a strong proxy for whether developers would champion Snyk internally after the pilot.

The question (exactly one)

"On a scale of 0–10, how likely are you to recommend Snyk to a colleague?"
0 = Not at all likely · 10 = Extremely likely

Grouping of respondents

ScoreCategoryMeaning
9–10PromotersEnthusiastic; will advocate for adoption
7–8PassivesSatisfied but unenthusiastic; excluded from the formula
0–6DetractorsUnhappy; will resist or bad-mouth the rollout

Calculation

NPS = (% of Promoters) − (% of Detractors) · range: −100 to +100

Worked example (all 40 pilot participants respond): 18 Promoters (45%), 14 Passives (35%), 8 Detractors (20%):

NPS = 45% − 20% = +25 → meets our target of ≥ 20

Interpretation benchmarks

NPSReading
< 0More detractors than promoters — serious adoption risk
0–20Acceptable; lukewarm
20–50Good (our pilot target: ≥ 20)
> 50Excellent; strong internal champions likely

Administration in this pilot: included as a single question in the W4 survey, answered once per participant, scored on the whole Snyk experience (CLI + IDE combined). Optional follow-up: "What is the main reason for your score?" (free text, used in debrief).

B.2 SUS — System Usability Scale

What it is: SUS is a standardized 10-item usability questionnaire created by John Brooke (Digital Equipment Corporation, 1986). Despite its age it remains the most widely used quick usability instrument because it is technology-agnostic, reliable even with small samples (works with n ≥ ~12), and produces a single 0–100 score with well-published benchmarks.

The 10 standard statements

Each is answered on a 5-point Likert scale: 1 = Strongly disagree … 5 = Strongly agree. The wording alternates positive/negative on purpose (reduces acquiescence bias).

  1. I think that I would like to use this system frequently.
  2. I found the system unnecessarily complex.
  3. I thought the system was easy to use.
  4. I think that I would need the support of a technical person to be able to use this system.
  5. I found the various functions in this system were well integrated.
  6. I thought there was too much inconsistency in this system.
  7. I would imagine that most people would learn to use this system very quickly.
  8. I found the system very cumbersome to use.
  9. I felt very confident using the system.
  10. I needed to learn a lot of things before I could get going with this system.

Calculation (per respondent)

Odd items (1,3,5,7,9): contribution = response − 1
Even items (2,4,6,8,10): contribution = 5 − response
SUS score = (sum of all 10 contributions) × 2.5 · range: 0–100

Worked example: a participant answers Q1=4, Q2=2, Q3=5, Q4=1, Q5=4, Q6=2, Q7=4, Q8=1, Q9=5, Q10=2:

Odd: (4−1)+(5−1)+(4−1)+(4−1)+(5−1) = 3+4+3+3+4 = 17
Even: (5−2)+(5−1)+(5−2)+(5−1)+(5−2) = 3+4+3+4+3 = 17
SUS = (17 + 17) × 2.5 = 85 → excellent usability

Group score: average the individual SUS scores across respondents (do not average the raw item answers first).

Interpretation benchmarks (published norms, ~68 = historical average)

SUSReading
< 50Poor — significant usability problems
50–68Marginal — below average
68–80Good — above average (our pilot target: ≥ 68)
> 80Excellent — users can self-serve

Administration in this pilot: the 10 items are answered twice per participant in W4 — once with "the system" = Snyk CLI, once with "the system" = Snyk IDE plugin — so CLI and IDE get separate SUS scores (SC-7 requires ≥ 68 for each). Participants who only used one surface answer only that version. "System" in each item is replaced by "the Snyk CLI" / "the Snyk IDE plugin" respectively.

B.3 Why both?

  • SUS measures usability (can people use it effectively?) — diagnostic for UX friction.
  • NPS measures advocacy (will people push for it?) — predictive of rollout success.
  • Together they separate the two classic pilot failure modes: "usable but nobody cares" (high SUS, low NPS → value-positioning problem) and "valuable but painful" (low SUS, high NPS → UX/education problem, fixable in rollout).
